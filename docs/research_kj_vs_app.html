Adopt the KJ checklist, or keep the engine?

A component-by-component review of the Kiran Jadhav & Associates trading checklist against this app's existing prediction engine, weighed against the published technical-analysis literature. Every claim is cited; confidence is labelled.

How to read this document. It was compiled without live web access, so it cites canonical, verifiable books and peer-reviewed papers (full list at the end). Where a specific statistic is recalled from memory rather than re-checked against the source, it is tagged [verify]. Confidence in each conclusion is labelled strong, mixed, or weak based on how consistent the evidence is. Nothing here is investment advice.

Bottom line first

Recommendation: keep the current engine as the core, selectively adopt two cheap KJ ideas, and skip the rest.

  • Keep the app's ATR-based stops, pivot/swing/VWAP support-resistance, and probabilistic multi-signal synthesis — each is better supported by evidence than the KJ equivalent.
  • Consider adopting (a) a fast EMA crossover as one more input (not a standalone verdict), and (b) making the R:R floor an explicit, surfaced discipline — the app already computes R:R, so this is nearly free.
  • Skip the naive "count B vs S" verdict tally, the candle-mean support/resistance definition, and mean-based stops. Do not over-weight candlestick patterns.

Why: the KJ checklist is a training firm's pedagogical system — its strength is being simple enough to teach, not statistical edge. The app's design choices line up better with what the literature actually supports. Details below.

1. SMA vs EMA verdict: roughly a tie

Textbooks agree on the mechanics: an EMA weights recent prices more heavily (weight α = 2/(n+1)), so it turns faster and lags less than an SMA of the same length; an SMA is smoother and produces fewer false flips.1, 2 That part is not controversial — it is arithmetic.

The important question is whether the choice of averaging scheme changes returns. The most thorough modern study of exactly this — Zakamulin's book-length treatment of moving-average market timing — concludes that SMA, EMA, and other weighting schemes produce statistically indistinguishable performance once you hold the lookback window fixed; what matters is the window length and the market regime, not SMA-vs-EMA.3 He also shows that much of the apparent out-performance of any specific MA rule is an artifact of data mining across the many possible parameter choices.3, 4

For us: arguing SMA-vs-EMA in the abstract is close to a wash. The app already uses both (SMA for position-vs-trend, EMA for the faster crossover), which is a defensible hedge. Switching everything to EMA would not, on the evidence, be an improvement.

2. MA crossovers: fast (5-13-26) vs slow (50/200) verdict: mixed

Do MA crossovers work at all? The landmark study is Brock, Lakonishok & LeBaron (1992): testing moving-average and trading-range-breakout rules on ~90 years of the Dow, they found buy signals were followed by higher average returns and lower volatility than sell signals, with statistically significant differences.5 strong, but…

…the caveat is large. Sullivan, Timmermann & White (1999) re-examined the same rule universe with White's "Reality Check" bootstrap to correct for data-snooping, and found the best rule's superiority weakens sharply out-of-sample (post-1986) and much of the edge may be spurious once you account for how many rules were tried.6 Broad surveys since then (Park & Irwin's review of ~95 modern studies) find a majority report some positive results, but consistently flag data-snooping, transaction costs, and ex-post rule selection as reasons to be cautious.7 [verify: ~56 of 95 positive]

Fast vs slow specifically. Shorter pairs (KJ's 5-13 / 5-26) react sooner but generate many more whipsaws in choppy markets; longer pairs (the app's 50/200 "Golden/Death Cross") are slower but filter noise. This is the classic responsiveness-vs-reliability trade-off described in every standard text,1, 8 and Zakamulin's data-mining warning applies with extra force to short windows, where the number of false signals — and the temptation to curve-fit — is highest.3 Transaction costs and slippage also bite short-window systems harder because they trade more often.9

For us: a fast EMA crossover is a legitimate additional signal, especially for short (daily/weekly) horizons — but as one weighted input, not a standalone buy/sell trigger. The app already runs EMA-9/21 (close in spirit to 5-13). Adding 5-13/5-26 is low-cost and defensible, provided it feeds the probabilistic synthesis rather than overriding it. Do not expect a fast crossover to beat the slower one net of whipsaws and costs.

3. Candlestick patterns verdict: weak evidence

Candlesticks are beautifully documented by Nison, who introduced them to Western audiences.10 But the profitability evidence is poor. Marshall, Young & Rose tested the full candlestick catalogue on the Dow and found the signals did not generate value for investors after accounting for the market; follow-up work in other markets reached similar null results.11, 12 Lo, Mamaysky & Wang — generally sympathetic to pattern recognition — focused on chart patterns rather than single candles, and even there the information content was modest.13

For us: the app already covers all 61 TA-Lib candlestick patterns (a superset of KJ's list). That is more than enough — arguably already too much weight on a weakly-supported family. No reason to elevate candlesticks further; treating them as minor, confirmation-only inputs (as the engine does) matches the evidence.

4. Chart patterns (H&S, double top, wedges, flags) verdict: mixed, pattern-dependent

Lo, Mamaysky & Wang built an automated kernel-regression detector for classic patterns and found several — notably head-and-shoulders and double bottoms — carry incremental, statistically detectable information, mostly on NASDAQ stocks, though the economic magnitude was small.13 Bulkowski's encyclopedic hand-measured statistics rank patterns by reliability and repeatedly show that flags, pennants and wedges are among the least reliable (often below 50% "meets target"), while head-and-shoulders and double tops/bottoms rank higher.14

For us: this directly justifies the app's current choices. It detects the high-reliability patterns (H&S, inverse H&S, double top/bottom) and deliberately omits the low-reliability wedges and flags that KJ lists — because they lack a robust closed-form definition and Bulkowski rates them poorly. Adding wedges/flags would import KJ's weakest items. Recommend: skip, or add only with explicit low weight and a reliability caveat.

5. Support / resistance definition verdict: app's method is better founded

KJ defines support/resistance from a single candle (mean of a green candle / low of a red one, and vice-versa). There is no literature supporting a one-candle definition as meaningful S/R. The concepts with actual empirical and practitioner backing are swing highs/lows, prior 52-week extremes, floor-trader pivot points, and VWAP — exactly what the app computes.1, 15 Trading-range-breakout rules (which depend on multi-bar S/R, not one candle) are the very rules BLLB found predictive.5 Pivot points have a long floor-trading pedigree and a standard published formula set.15

For us: keep the app's S/R stack. The KJ single-candle definition is the weakest part of its checklist and has no evidentiary support. Do not adopt it.

6. Signal aggregation: tally vs probability verdict: app's approach is better

KJ's verdict rule is a raw tally: count Buy rows vs Sell rows, output B / S / Indecision. This treats every signal as equally informative and independent — both assumptions are false (indicators built on the same price series are highly correlated, and their reliabilities differ wildly, as sections 3–4 show). The evidence-based-TA literature is explicit that naive equal-weight rule stacking inflates false confidence and is highly vulnerable to data-snooping.4, 6 Weighting signals by reliability and expressing the output as a calibrated probability (which the app does, and then scores with Brier/skill metrics) is the methodologically sounder approach.4

For us: the app's weighted, calibrated, multi-source synthesis is a strict methodological upgrade over a row count. Keep it. Replacing it with KJ's tally would be a step backwards.

7. Stop-loss placement: candle-mean vs ATR verdict: ATR is better founded

KJ places the stop at the candle mean. The app places it at a volatility-scaled distance (a multiple of Average True Range, per horizon). ATR-based stops trace directly to Wilder, who introduced ATR precisely so that stop distance adapts to each instrument's volatility instead of using an arbitrary fixed level.16 Volatility-based stops (e.g. chandelier / ATR stops) are standard in the systematic-trading literature.8, 17 A candle-mean stop ignores volatility entirely: it will be far too tight on a wide-range stock and too loose on a quiet one.

For us: keep ATR stops. This is one of the clearest wins for the current engine.

8. The "minimum 1:3" reward-to-risk rule verdict: useful discipline, incomplete on its own

A fixed R:R floor is a sound risk-management habit, but by itself it says nothing about whether a system makes money. What determines profitability is expectancy = (win-rate × average win) − (loss-rate × average loss). A 1:3 setup with a 20% hit rate loses money; a 1:1 setup at 60% makes money. Van Tharp's position-sizing framework makes exactly this point: R:R must be paired with win-rate to be meaningful.18 Insisting on 1:3 also biases you toward far-away targets that get hit less often — the trade-off is real, not free.

For us: the app already computes and displays a (worst-fill) R:R. Cheap, evidence-consistent upgrade: surface it more prominently and optionally flag (not silently reject) setups below a chosen R:R floor, while continuing to track realised win-rate so expectancy — not R:R alone — is the real gate.
