Adopt the KJ checklist, or keep the engine?

A component-by-component review of the Kiran Jadhav & Associates trading checklist against this app's prediction engine, weighed against the published technical-analysis literature. Sources were verified live against Crossref and OpenAlex on 2026-07-19; each is labelled by verification status.

Bottom line

Keep the current engine as the core. Selectively adopt two cheap KJ ideas. Skip the rest.

  • Keep ATR-based stops, pivot/swing/VWAP support-resistance, and the probabilistic multi-signal synthesis.
  • Consider adopting (a) a fast EMA crossover as one weighted input, and (b) surfacing the reward-to-risk ratio as an explicit discipline (the app already computes it).
  • Skip the count-the-rows verdict, the single-candle support/resistance definition, and the candle-mean stop. Do not over-weight candlesticks; do not add wedges/flags.

The strongest, most consistent finding across the verified literature is a cautionary one: apparent technical-trading edges shrink substantially once you correct for data-snooping, transaction costs, and look-ahead bias.2, 3, 5 That argues for humility in any rule system—and for the app's calibrated, probabilistic framing over a deterministic tally.

1. SMA vs EMA

The mechanical difference is arithmetic, not empirical: an EMA weights recent prices more heavily and therefore lags less than an equal-length SMA, at the cost of more false flips; an SMA is smoother. This is standard textbook material.10, 11 textbook

The deeper and more important verified finding is that moving-average market timing itself barely outperforms buy-and-hold once methodological errors are removed. Zakamulin (2018) re-examined a widely-cited result and showed its stellar performance was a look-ahead-bias artifact:

"...we demonstrate that this ‘too good to be true’ reported performance of the moving average strategy is due to simulating trading with look-ahead bias... the performance of the moving average strategy is indistinguishable from the performance of the buy-and-hold strategy."5 verified verbatim
For us: the SMA-vs-EMA sub-choice is second-order. The app already uses both (SMA for trend position, EMA for the faster cross), which is a reasonable hedge. Switching everything to EMA is not, on the evidence, an improvement worth making.

2. MA crossovers: fast (5-13-26) vs slow (50/200)

The foundational pro-crossover result is Brock, Lakonishok & LeBaron (1992):

"This paper tests two of the simplest and most popular trading rules—moving average and trading range break—by utilizing the Dow Jones Index from 1897 to 1986... Overall, our results provide strong support for the technical strategies. Buy signals consistently generate higher returns than sell signals..."1 verified verbatim (cited ~1,580×)

The decisive caveat is Sullivan, Timmermann & White (1999), who re-ran BLLB's rule universe with a data-snooping correction:

"...we utilize White's Reality Check bootstrap methodology... quantifying the data-snooping bias and fully adjusting for its effect... We consider the study of Brock, Lakonishok, and LeBaron (1992), expand their universe of 26 trading rules, apply the rules to 100 years of daily data on the Dow Jones Industrial Average, and determine the effects of data-snooping."2 verified verbatim

STW's widely-reported conclusion is that the best rule's superiority is much weaker—and largely not robust—out-of-sample once data-snooping is accounted for. metadata verified; conclusion is the paper's well-documented result

And the broad survey, Park & Irwin (2007), quantifies the balance of evidence:

"Among a total of 95 modern studies, 56 studies find positive results regarding technical trading strategies, 20 studies obtain negative results, and 19 studies indicate mixed results... most empirical studies are subject to various problems in their testing procedures, e.g. data snooping, ex post selection of trading rules... and difficulties in estimation of risk and transaction costs."3 verified verbatim

On fast vs slow specifically: shorter pairs (KJ's 5-13/5-26) react sooner but whipsaw more in choppy markets and trade more often, so transaction costs bite harder; longer pairs (the app's 50/200) filter noise but lag. This responsiveness-vs-cost trade-off is standard, 10, 12 and the filter-rule literature dating to Fama & Blume (1966) established that frequent-trading rules tend not to beat buy-and-hold after costs.7 verified verbatim (Fama-Blume abstract)

For us: a fast EMA crossover is a legitimate additional input, especially at short horizons—but as a weighted signal feeding the synthesis, never a standalone trigger. The app already runs EMA-9/21 (close in spirit to 5-13). Adding 5-13/5-26 is low-cost and defensible; just don't expect it to beat the slower cross net of whipsaws and costs.

3. Candlestick patterns

Candlesticks were popularised in the West by Nison.13 textbook The most-cited profitability test is Marshall, Young & Rose (2006), "Candlestick technical trading strategies: Can they create value for investors?", Journal of Banking & Finance 30(8):2303-2323.6

Honesty flag. I confirmed this paper exists (DOI + ~106-141 citations) but could not retrieve its abstract from Crossref or OpenAlex this session, so I am not quoting its conclusion. Its widely-reported result is that candlestick signals do not create value for Dow stocks—but treat that as unverified here. could not verify finding
For us: regardless, the app already covers all 61 TA-Lib candlestick patterns (a superset of KJ's list). Treating them as minor confirmation-only inputs is prudent; there is no case for elevating them.

4. Chart patterns (H&S, double top/bottom, wedges, flags)

Lo, Mamaysky & Wang (2000) built an automated detector and tested classic patterns on U.S. stocks 1962-1996:

"...conditioned on specific technical indicators such as head-and-shoulders or double bottoms—we find that over the 31-year sample period, several technical indicators do provide incremental information and may have some practical value."4 verified verbatim (cited ~810×)

Note the calibrated wording: "incremental information" and "some practical value"—detectable, but modest. On the specific patterns KJ lists, the practitioner reference (Bulkowski) ranks flags, pennants and wedges among the least reliable, and head-and-shoulders / double tops-bottoms higher.14 textbook — exact reliability percentages not re-verified this session

For us: this supports the app's current choices exactly—it detects the higher-information patterns (H&S, inverse H&S, double top/bottom) and omits wedges/flags. Adding wedges/flags would import KJ's weakest items. Recommend: skip, or add only at low weight with a reliability caveat.

5. Support / resistance definition

KJ defines support/resistance from a single candle. There is no literature supporting a one-candle definition as meaningful S/R. The concepts with empirical and practitioner backing are multi-bar: swing highs/lows, prior extremes, floor-trader pivot points, and VWAP—which is exactly what the app computes.10, 15 Crucially, the "trading range break" rule that BLLB found predictive is built on multi-bar support/resistance, not one candle.1 verified verbatim (BLLB) pivot formulas: textbook

For us: keep the app's S/R stack. The KJ single-candle definition has no evidentiary support—do not adopt it.

6. Signal aggregation: tally vs calibrated probability

KJ's verdict is a raw tally (count Buy rows vs Sell rows). This treats every signal as equally informative and independent—both false, since indicators on the same price series are correlated and (per sections 3-4) differ hugely in reliability. Park & Irwin's verified survey names data snooping and ex-post rule selection as the dominant hazards of technical rule systems3—hazards a naive equal-weight tally does nothing to control. Weighting by reliability and outputting a calibrated probability (then scoring it) is the sounder design.8 Aronson: textbook argument

For us: the app's weighted, calibrated, multi-source synthesis is a methodological upgrade over a row count. Replacing it with KJ's tally would be a step backwards.

7. Stop-loss placement: candle-mean vs ATR

KJ places the stop at the candle mean—a fixed level that ignores volatility. The app uses a multiple of Average True Range per horizon. ATR was introduced by Wilder precisely so stop distance adapts to each instrument's volatility; volatility-scaled stops are standard in the systematic-trading literature.16, 12 textbook A candle-mean stop will be far too tight on a wide-range stock and too loose on a quiet one.

For us: keep ATR stops—one of the clearest wins for the current engine.

8. The "minimum 1:3" reward-to-risk rule

A fixed R:R floor is a sound risk-management habit but says nothing about profitability on its own. What determines that is expectancy = (win-rate × average win) − (loss-rate × average loss): a 1:3 setup at a 20% hit rate loses money; a 1:1 setup at 60% makes money. Insisting on 1:3 also biases toward distant targets that are hit less often. Van Tharp's position-sizing framework makes exactly this pairing of R:R with win-rate central.17 textbook

For us: the app already computes and displays a (worst-fill) R:R. Cheap, evidence-consistent upgrade: surface it prominently and flag (don't silently reject) sub-floor setups, while tracking realised win-rate so expectancy—not R:R alone—is the real gate.
